<small>*The prefix “exa” refers to the number one quintillion (10*<sup>18</sup>*). In the context of computing, an exascale computer can do one quintillion, or a billion billion, calculations per second.*</small>

The research group I am doing my Ph.D. in both studies uses supercomputers. The area is sometimes also referred to as supercomputing or high-performance computing. Historically, one of the main objectives of supercomputing was to enable and accelerate computational science using really fast computers. For example, simulating galaxies, forecasting weather, or even modeling the movement of molecules requires enormous amounts of calculations, which wouldn’t be possible on personal computers (at least not if we wanted the calculations to finish within our lifetimes). 

**In this blog, I will recount how the abilities and use cases of supercomputers have evolved over the last few decades. I will then discuss how I approach and think about supercomputing in modern day as it relates to my dissertation research in machine learning. I will conclude by laying the groundwork for a future vision in which computational science remains open and serves the public good.**

## So what is a (modern) supercomputer?

Seymour Cray, “the father of supercomputing,” liked to ask:

> “If you were plowing a field, which would you rather use? Two strong oxen or 1024 chickens?”

Cray began his career making the case for the<a href="https://snormore.dev/blog/the-harness-problem/" target="_blank">oxen</a><sup>3</sup>: he argued that by making one ultra fast computer (oxen) one could out-perform several slower computers (chickens) working together. And he did alright with this bet…for a while. But by the mid 1990s, the oxen argument was falling apart.

**It turns out that if you need to do a bunch of calculations as fast as possible, multiple ~~chickens~~ computers working together is the most simple and efficient solution.** This is because with multiple computers you can divide and conquer the computations: computations that don’t rely on each other can be run at the same time on smaller computers (called *nodes*) in parallel rather than waiting to be run one at a time on one big computer.

Today, the chicken argument has won. All modern supercomputers are designed to be parallel machines. During my Ph.D., I have seen large scale computers come online, be decommissioned, and proposed for future planning. One of the most notable unveilings happened in 2023 when<a href="https://top500.org/news/frontier-remains-no-1-in-the-top500-but-aurora-with-intels-sapphire-rapids-chips-enters-with-a-half-scale-system-at-no-2/" target="_blank">Aurora</a><sup>4</sup> came online. Aurora debuted as the world’s second exascale computer, meaning that it could do a quintillion calculations per second. This meant that the world had gone from petascale (think IBM Blue Gene) to exascale in less than two decades…in other words, we can now do 1,000 trillion more computations per second than previous-generation supercomputers.

## My Adventures in Supercomputing

I was lucky enough to be one of the first researchers on Aurora. At the time, I was working on a large-scale machine learning research project in which I was studying how neural networks can work together to learn over distributed datasets. I used Aurora to train tens of thousands of neural networks to understand how information was learned and propagated. Compared to my previous projects, the size of my experiments increased by orders of magnitude overnight. And I wasn’t the only one: Aurora was being used to accelerate computational research on fusion energy and plasma physics, materials science, cosmology, and nanotechnology, to name a few.

I quickly realized that as the scale of my experiments increased, so did the problems. The speed at which parallel processes were trying to run computations and save the results raised two very specific issues:
1. When you try to save ALL of your parallel experimental results across hundreds of thousands of experiments through a single node that is coordinating the experiments, it can cause massive slowdowns. 
2. When tons of computations are being run on a node, it generates heat. And when a node gets too hot, it gets fried.

I had been warned about the difficulties of parallel debugging in my undergraduate course work; I had even debugged parallel code on my laptop before. But never before had I done it at such a large scale on a brand new system. 

Luckily, some colleagues came to my aid! With a little elbow grease, I learned how to refactor my experiments to make better use of Aurora’s parallelism: this meant that any experiments that were not dependent on each other had to be runnable (**and savable!**) at the same time across many nodes, and even within an experiment, any procedures that were independent were designed to be able to run at the same time on a single node across multiple GPUs. These simple tweaks solved problem #1 because now I was able to save information from many different nodes rather than being bottlenecked by one. 

Problem #2 quickly resolved itself as the kinks in Aurora’s cooling system got worked out by the amazing researchers and staff who built and maintained the machine. Turns out, Aurora was one of the first large-scale public machines to use a liquid cooling system. This meant there were additional engineering challenges when designing and implementing this cooling system, since Aurora has a massive power draw, but it ended up being substantially more efficient and effective than traditional fan-based air cooling.

Today, thanks to the amazing assistance that the Aurora staff provide scientists in adapting their experimental code, and their fast response to any hardware issues, Aurora has become an amazing resource for computational science workloads.
